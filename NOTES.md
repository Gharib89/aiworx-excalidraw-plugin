# Graph-drawing aesthetic criteria: evidence, metrics, computability (#184)

Research notes for wayfinder ticket [#184](https://github.com/Gharib89/aiworx-excalidraw-plugin/issues/184), child of map [#183](https://github.com/Gharib89/aiworx-excalidraw-plugin/issues/183). Sources are primary (the papers themselves, or the tool's own reference docs); each claim carries its source. "Computable today" is judged against `tools/geometry.js` (`outline`, `bounds`, `gap`, `segmentGap`, `clearance`, `outlineContains`, `shapeDepth`, `segmentLengthInsideShape`, and the module-private `segmentsCross` / `pointToSegment`) and against what `tools/verify.js` already walks.

## 1. Ranked criteria

Ranking is by strength of **performance** evidence (time/error in controlled experiments) first, preference and design-practice evidence second. The plugin's regime is small diagrams (a sketch is ≤ ~80 elements; a band frame holds one idea), which is exactly the regime where the small-graph studies apply and the large-graph caveats do not.

Also in the classical lists but not ranked above: **area / width** (73 % preferred the narrower class diagram, 57 % the wider collaboration diagram — Purchase et al. 2002; "keep edges short", `dot` A3 — Gansner et al. 1993) and **aspect ratio** (match the container — Taylor & Rodgers 2005 via Bennett; the Handbook lists it as a standard measure). The presets in `SKILL.md` already target an aspect; a clamp would be a design decision, not an evidence question.

## 2. Reading the ranking

- **Crossings and continuity trade against each other.** Ware's exchange rate (one crossing ≈ 38° of bend) is the only quantitative trade-off in the literature; `dot`'s A2 groups crossings and sharp bends as the same class of "visual anomaly". HOLA found humans *keep* a crossing or a bend to gain symmetry or compactness. So a refusing rule on either would be wrong; an advisory count with the trade-off stated is what the sources support.
- **Which crossings matter is task-relative.** Ware: total crossings in the drawing were not a significant predictor; crossings *on the path being read* were. A diagram that "argues" one claim (SKILL.md) has a spine; crossings on the spine cost, crossings between two side arrows barely do. The gate can only tell them apart if the author names the spine (the step-1 panel list is the natural place).
- **Small-graph results transfer; large-graph ones do not.** Kobourov 2014 (40 vs 120 nodes) and Eades et al. (blobs hide crossings) both put the boundary above anything a sketch or a frame holds. Every threshold above is a small-graph threshold.
- **Preference and performance disagree on orthogonality.** UML users *say* they want grids (61 %); on task the gridded layout with the fewest crossings did worst (Dwyer 2009), and forcing orthogonality drags every other metric (Mooney 2024). For this plugin the safe signal is the near-miss (almost-axis-aligned segment), not "not orthogonal".
- **Computed symmetry is not perceived symmetry.** Purchase et al. 2001 dropped their metric for this reason and hand-rated symmetry instead. Any symmetry number the gate emits is a hint for the self-critique step, not a finding.
- **Gestalt: connectedness and common region beat proximity and similarity.** Palmer 1992 and Palmer & Rock 1994 order the principles; for a scene that means a rectangle drawn around things groups them harder than spacing does, and an arrow between two boxes groups them harder than colour does. The corresponding defect classes are *unintended* common region (shape inside a container it does not belong to) and *contradicted* proximity (a group member farther from its group than an outsider is).

## 3. What this adds beyond the shipped skill

The baseline survey on #183 lists the composition rules the skill prescribes as prose and the 23 element-level codes `verify.js` refuses on. Against that:

**Already covered, now with its evidence attached.** `arrow-crossing` (arrow through a shape) is the pass-through half of node–edge proximity (#8); `text-struck-by-arrow` at 6 px is the text case of the same; `authoring.md`'s `originAt`/`landAt` split is angular resolution (#11) for the two-arrows-into-one-box case; "a cycle costs you the reading order" and `graph({direction})` are flow consistency (#4) for authored graphs; `unbound-over-frame` is common region (#9b) for frames; the "cramped panel beside an empty one" anti-pattern is node distribution (#10) stated qualitatively.

**Silent in the skill, measurable today (in the order the evidence ranks them):**

1. **Arrow–arrow crossings** (#1) — the single strongest criterion in the literature and the first item on the baseline's "silent" list. Needs `segmentsCross` exported and a polyline walker; everything else is present.
2. **Crossing angle** (#3) — trivial once crossings are found; 70° is a real threshold from the eye-tracking work.
3. **Continuity through a node and bends per arrow** (#2) — bindings give the path; `outline` gives the angles. Reportable as Ware's degrees-of-deviation with the 38°-per-crossing exchange rate in the message.
4. **Arrow near an unrelated shape** (#8) — `segmentGap` exists and is exported; the floor is a design choice (ELK's 10 px is the one documented default).
5. **Flow consistency and reverse edges** (#4) — direction vectors from `outline`; the back-edge list is more useful than the fraction.
6. **Orthogonality near-miss** (#6) and **alignment near-miss** (#9c) — cheap; label both as practice, since the literature does not supply the band.
7. **Proximity/common-region conflicts against `groupIds` and container rectangles** (#9a, #9b) — `gap` and `outlineContains` exist; the skill currently uses containers only as prose advice.
8. **Symmetry, edge-length CV, node-distribution CV** (#5, #7, #10) — computable but weak or contradictory evidence; if emitted at all, as numbers for the self-critique read-back, never as refusals.

**What the literature does not give:** pixel thresholds for proximity grouping, a crossing count that is "too many" for a drawing (only per-crossing costs), any performance evidence for even node distribution, and any validated model of perceived symmetry. Where the notes name a number for those, it is a tool default (ELK) or design practice, and marked so.

**Fits the gate's shape.** Every metric above is element-level geometry `verify.js` can walk in the loop it already runs; none needs a render. All of them are advisory by the evidence — the sources support cost estimates and trade-offs, not hard limits — so they belong beside the fidelity ledger as findings that do not refuse, not among the 23 refusing codes.
