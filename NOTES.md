# Practitioner diagramming conventions — research notes (#185)

Question: what do the widely-adopted practitioner sources prescribe for software /
architecture diagrams, and which of those rules is the shipped skill missing?

Baseline: the survey in the first comment of map issue #183 (2026-08-26). Status
marks are against that survey plus a re-read of `skills/excalidraw-diagram/SKILL.md`
and `reference/{patterns,anti-patterns,palette,authoring}.md` on `origin/main` at
`c1db420`. Vocabulary follows `CONTEXT.md` (sketch, band, panel, claim, register,
role, gate).

Status legend: **in skill** — prescribed as prose or enforced by the gate;
**partly** — a neighbouring rule exists but not this one; **missing** — the skill
is silent. Every rule is one checkable statement; "rationale" is the source's own.

## 1. Notation & legend

| # | Rule (checkable) | Source | Rationale | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Every diagram has a key/legend explaining every shape, colour, border style, line type and arrowhead used. | C4 Notation; C4 checklist ("Does the diagram have a key/legend?"); Brown 2020 (recommends a key even for UML/ArchiMate) | The diagram must stand alone without its author present; ad-hoc boxes-and-lines are otherwise undecodable. | **missing** — the skill has no legend convention; `problem-codes.md` only notes a legend "sits outside the band" as legal. The skill *depends* on one: `palette.md` keeps role names off cards, so blue/cyan/green/purple/gold/red is decodable only through a key that is never drawn. |
| 1.2 | Direct labels beat a legend: put words on the graphic itself so the eye never darts between key and picture; a legend is the fallback for encodings that cannot be labelled in place. | Tufte VDQI ch. 9 ("labels are placed on the graphic itself; no legend is required" vs "obscure codings require going back and forth between legend and graphic"; "integrate the caption and legend into the design") | Words on graphics are data-ink; back-and-forth decoding is cognitive cost. | **partly** — "show the thing, not its name" and edge labels in ink cover text; the colour roles are exactly the unlabelled encoding Tufte says needs either in-place labels or a key. Reconciled rule for the skill: colour/border/head encodings need a key **or** an in-place label; nothing encoded stays silent. |
| 1.3 | One graphical symbol ↔ one concept (no symbol overload: the same mark meaning two things; no redundancy: two marks for one thing). | Moody 2009, Principle of Semiotic Clarity; Wong, Color coding ("symbols have minimal overlapping meanings … arrows to indicate progression and leaders … for labeling") | Overload creates ambiguity; redundancy wastes the reader's symbol budget. | **partly** — "colour encodes one meaning each" is this rule for hue. Two overloads exist in the skill itself: `dashed` = "provisional or inferred" (patterns.md) **and** the stroke of a label leader (authoring.md); `grey` = scaffolding **and** labels **and** the depth-tier ghosts share the "de-emphasis" channel with opacity. |
| 1.4 | Same type of element → same shape, same size, same style, every time. | draw.io blog ("Use the same shape for the same type of element"; "Make all the elements … of the same type also the same size"); C4 checklist (element sizes must have a stated meaning); Bertin (size is *dissociative* and *ordered*: a bigger box reads as "more") | Consistency lets the reader pattern-match instead of re-decoding; size differences are perceived as ranking whether or not one was meant. | **missing** — the baseline lists uniform node size as silent. `box` sizes each card to its text, so a layer of cards with different label lengths ships at different sizes and reads as a ranking. |
| 1.5 | Keep the number of distinct symbol types cognitively manageable (~6); prefer a symbol deficit (some concepts shown as text) to a large vocabulary. | Moody 2009, Graphic Economy; Tufte EI ch. 5 ("more than 20 or 30 colors … negative returns"); Brown, notation tips (Miller's 7±2 for responsibilities) | Novices cannot discriminate or recall large symbol sets. | **in skill** — six colour roles + grey, seven shape meanings, six arrowheads; each vocabulary is within budget. No rule caps how many of them one *diagram* combines (baseline: colour-count limit silent). |
| 1.6 | Colour coding is made explicit and survives colour-vision deficiency and greyscale printing; red-vs-green is never an essential contrast. | C4 Notation ("colour-blindness and grayscale printing"); Brown notation tips; Tufte VDQI ch. 9 ("red and green used for essential contrasts" is *unfriendly*; blue is the safe hue) | 5–10 % of viewers are colour-deficient; printouts drop hue. | **missing / contradicted** — `palette.md` checks luminance contrast only. `pass` = green `#6E9A21`, `fail` = red `#B61E24`: the one contrast the skill most wants read is the one Tufte names as failing. Roles also differ by hue alone (no shape/border redundancy), so a greyscale export loses every role. |
| 1.7 | Symbols must be perceptually discriminable: differ on at least one visual variable by a large step, not a subtle one; shape alone is weakly discriminable. | Moody 2009, Perceptual Discriminability; Wong, Salience | Small visual distance → recognition errors, worse for novices. | **partly** — contrast floors and distinct hues do this for colour; nothing says a `rectangle` vs `ellipse` distinction is too small when both are card-sized. |
| 1.8 | Semantic transparency: prefer marks whose appearance suggests their meaning (cylinder = store, person = actor, funnel = aggregation). | Moody 2009, Semantic Transparency; Mermaid semantic shapes (v11.3: cylinder "Database storage", diamond "Decision") | Built-in mnemonics cut decoding load. | **in skill** — the whole of step 2 / patterns.md (shape-that-means-it, isomorphism test) is this principle, stated more strongly than any source. |
| 1.9 | Dual-code: text reinforces graphics — every element carries a name, a type (abstraction level) and a one-line description; every container/component names its technology. | C4 Notation; C4 checklist (Elements section); Moody 2009, Dual Coding; Tufte VDQI ch. 9 ("Words and pictures belong together") | Text and image are stored separately in working memory; together they disambiguate. | **partly** — names and concrete content ("show the thing") yes; element *type* and *technology* lines are absent, because the skill was written for teaching bands, not C4. An architecture sketch drawn with the skill will lack the `[Container: Spring Boot]` line C4 asks for. |
| 1.10 | Acronyms and abbreviations are spelled out or explained in the key. | C4 Notation; C4 checklist; Brown notation tips; Tufte VDQI ch. 9 ("words are spelled out") | New joiners and outside audiences cannot decode internal names. | **missing**. |
| 1.11 | A hand-drawn/"sketchy" rendering signals *draft, not final* to architecture audiences. | C4-PlantUML `LAYOUT_AS_SKETCH()` (adds a footer warning, exists to mark work-in-progress); Brown ("sticky notes and index cards … especially early on") | Readers infer finish from finish. | **contradicted by design** — `roughness: 1` is the house voice for *finished* work. A deliberate choice, but the skill should say the audience it costs: a C4-literate reader reads the house register as unfinished. |

## 2. Titles & captions

| # | Rule | Source | Rationale | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Every diagram has a title stating diagram type and scope ("System Context diagram for X"). | C4 Notation; C4 checklist; Brown 2020 ("upwards of 90 % of the initial diagrams … don't have a title") | Type + scope tell the reader what they are zooming into before they read a box. | **missing** — a band's frames are "named for the claim", which is a per-panel title; nothing asks for a diagram-level title, and a sketch has no title convention at all. |
| 2.2 | Titles are short, meaningful, and numbered when diagram order matters. | Brown, notation tips | Ordered reading across a set needs an explicit index. | **partly** — `render.js` numbers frame PNGs in reading order; frames themselves are not asked to carry a number. |
| 2.3 | Document the evidence: title, authors/sponsors, sources, scales, and (for architecture) *as of when*. | Tufte BE, Fundamental Principles ("Thoroughly describe the evidence"); C4 FAQ (diagrams go stale at different rates per level) | Un-dated architecture diagrams cannot be trusted; un-sourced charts cannot be checked. | **missing** — no footer/date/source convention. The skill does require chart numbers to be real, which is the integrity half. |
| 2.4 | The caption states the takeaway; annotate outliers and key points on the plotting field. | Tufte VDQI ch. 9 ("little messages help explain data"); BE ("completely integrate words, numbers, images") | Explanation on the graphic is data-ink; scattered text is not. | **in skill** — step 1's one-line claim per panel and "show the thing" cover it; authoring.md even routes a cycle's lost order into "the caption". No rule on *where* a caption sits (baseline: label placement silent). |
| 2.5 | Text runs left→right, one direction; upper-and-lower case. | Tufte VDQI ch. 9 (friendly table) | Rotated and multi-direction text is unfriendly. | **partly** — the gate refuses several text collisions but permits rotated text (rotation is in the geometry rules, not forbidden). |

## 3. Abstraction level & scope

| # | Rule | Source | Rationale | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Every element on one diagram sits at the same abstraction level; a diagram has one scope and one primary audience. | draw.io blog ("all diagram elements are at the same level of abstraction"); C4 diagram pages (each names *scope*, *primary elements*, *supporting elements*, *intended audience*); C4 checklist ("Do you understand the type of every element? (i.e. the level of abstraction …)") | Mixed zoom ("a whole company next to a Java class, same size, same colour") is why nobody can read the diagram; each level tells a different story to a different audience. | **partly** — "one idea per frame" and step 1's panel list carry the *scope* half. Nothing names *abstraction level* or asks for it to be uniform within a panel. |
| 3.2 | Supporting elements appear only when directly connected to the scope; external systems are not decomposed. | C4 System context / Container pages; Roos 2025 (secondary: "detailing external containers … creates unwanted coupling") | Detail outside the scope belongs to another diagram. | **missing**. |
| 3.3 | Deployment/infrastructure detail (clusters, load balancers, replication) stays off structural diagrams and goes to a deployment diagram per environment. | C4 Container page | It varies per environment and would multiply the picture. | **missing** (out of the skill's stated remit, but architecture requests will hit it). |
| 3.4 | Runtime sequence is shown with numbered interactions, sparingly, for one feature/use case. | C4 Dynamic page ("numbered interactions to indicate ordering"; "should be used sparingly") | Order cannot be read from spatial position on a collaboration-style picture. | **partly** — timeline/cycle patterns exist; no numbering convention for arrows. |
| 3.5 | Manage complexity explicitly: decompose into linked diagrams rather than one big one; keep per-diagram element counts small. | Moody 2009, Complexity Management & Cognitive Integration; C4 zoom levels | Visual representations do not scale; readers need a map between diagrams. | **in skill** — the sketch ceiling (~80 elements) and the band's frame-per-idea are this, with a numeric limit the sources lack. Cross-diagram navigation (how frame N points at the sketch it zooms) is silent. |

## 4. Arrows & lines

| # | Rule | Source | Rationale | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Every line is unidirectional; no bidirectional or headless relationship lines on architecture diagrams. | C4 Notation ("Every line should represent a unidirectional relationship"); Structurizr Notation ("Bidirectional … are ambiguous, and are not supported"); Brown 2020 ("harder to label correctly") | A two-way arrow cannot carry one verb; there is always an upstream. | **partly / tension** — patterns.md lists `null` heads as "a plain connector — relation without direction". Fine for a Venn or a tree trunk; wrong for a relationship. The skill does not say which. |
| 4.2 | Every arrow has a label naming the intent, in the direction drawn, specific enough to read aloud ("reads customer records from", not "uses"); inter-process lines also name protocol/technology. | C4 Notation; C4 checklist ("Does the description match the relationship direction?"); Brown notation tips | An unlabelled arrow "is a question mark that looks like an answer". | **partly** — the mechanism exists (`label:` with ink) but no rule requires a label or bans "uses". |
| 4.3 | One diagram uses one relationship semantics — dependency *or* data flow — and says which. | C4 FAQ ("choose whichever … ensure arrow directions align with descriptions") | Mixing "depends on" arrows with "sends data to" arrows on one picture makes direction unreadable. | **missing**. |
| 4.4 | Arrowheads are reserved for direction/sequence/causation; leaders (pointing at a label) have no head or a bullet; structural (non-functional) links are lines, not arrows. | Wong, Arrows 2011 ("reserve … arrow[heads] for indicating direction or sequence"; "Leaders … should have either no head or only a bullet"); Wong, Color coding | Arrows are read as change/motion/causality (readers given arrows report twice the functional content), so a decorative arrow asserts a process that is not there. | **in skill** — shape table (`line` = structure, `arrow` = "a real relationship") and headless dashed leaders match. The arrowhead vocabulary (diamond, circle, bar) is UML-ish and fine when the diagram *is* UML; patterns.md already warns against mixing heads for decoration. |
| 4.5 | Arrows are well-proportioned, avoid open "V" heads (trapped-whitespace sparkle), keep whitespace at both ends, and run in similar directions to form one visual flow. | Wong, Arrows 2011 | Discriminability and a continuous wireframe the eye can follow. | **in skill** — `standoff` and the finish register (Excalidraw's `arrow` head is filled/closed at `roughness 1`); "orienting arrows in similar directions" is implicit in "one reading direction". |
| 4.6 | Line style and colour on relationships each mean exactly one thing, stated in the key (e.g. solid = sync, dashed = async). | C4 checklist (Relationships section); draw.io blog ("different connector styles to indicate different groupings") | Same reason as 1.3, applied to edges. | **partly** — dashed = provisional is fixed diagram-wide, which is good; nothing lets an author declare a *different* edge-style meaning and record it. |

## 5. Reading order & layout

| # | Rule | Source | Rationale | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Sequential/process content flows left→right, hierarchical/dependency content top→bottom; the tools' defaults encode this (Graphviz `rankdir=TB`, Mermaid `TB`, C4-PlantUML `LAYOUT_TOP_DOWN` default, Structurizr auto-layout TB). | Graphviz `rankdir` docs; Mermaid flowchart docs; C4-PlantUML README; draw.io blog ("flow from left to right … top to bottom to match a user's natural reading flow in the West") | Matches the reading direction of the audience; against it the reader has to hunt for the start. | **in skill** — bands read left→right, `graph` defaults `down`, "one entry point, one reading direction". Not stated: *which* direction suits *which* content (sequence vs hierarchy), and that a sketch needs an entry point too (baseline: sketch reading order silent). |
| 5.2 | Most important element in the middle (or at the entry point), consistent orientation across a set (e.g. users top, database bottom on every diagram). | Brown, notation tips ("Most important thing in the middle; be consistent across diagrams"; "Users at the top and database at the bottom?") | Consistent orientation across diagrams lets a reader carry a mental map from one to the next. | **missing** — position semantics are silent; frames may place the same actor anywhere from panel to panel. |
| 5.3 | Layout plans the eye's journey: the element to read first has dominance (size, contrast, or surrounding whitespace); equal visual weight for everything means no entry point. | Wong, Layout 2011 ("make clear what is to be read first, second and so on"; "Without contrast, you're dead"); Wong, Salience to relevance (what stands out is taken as important) | Salience must align with relevance or attention lands on the wrong thing. | **in skill** — "whitespace around the element that matters most", opacity tiers, type ramp. No check that the most *salient* element is the *claim* (a bright `fail` card in a supporting corner wins the eye). |
| 5.4 | Group by Gestalt cues — proximity, similarity, enclosure, connection — and mean the grouping you draw; a border activates the negative space around its contents. | Wong, Gestalt principles 2010; Tufte EI ch. 3 ("1 + 1 = 3": two marks make a third; "avoid surrounding words by little boxes") | Readers group whatever is near/alike/enclosed whether the author intended it or not. | **partly** — "containers, sparingly" and "free-floating text is the default" are Tufte's box rule; Gestalt is not named (baseline), and nothing warns that an even grid of unrelated cards reads as one group. |
| 5.5 | Align to a grid; equal spacing between peers; symmetric margins. | draw.io blog (grid/snap, "neatly aligned and equally spaced"); Wong, Layout (grids "build consistency … the audience can anticipate where content will appear") | Alignment is how the eye reads order without counting. | **partly** — `stack`/`column`/`row` produce equal gaps inside one group; nothing aligns groups to each other or checks margin symmetry (baseline: whitespace balance, grid alignment silent). |
| 5.6 | Small multiples: repeat one design at postage-stamp size, same scale and axes, so comparison happens within the eyespan. | Tufte EI ch. 4; VDQI ch. 9 ("Comparison must be enforced within the scope of the eyespan") | "Compared with what?" is the fundamental analytical question; shared design makes the differing panel pop. | **partly** — "side by side, same size, one visual difference" is a two-cell small multiple. The rule "consecutive panels take different patterns" is its *opposite* and is right for a teaching band; the skill should say when the repeated-design case (a scale of variants) wins. |
| 5.7 | Minimise edge crossings and bends; route edges so they do not cut through unrelated nodes; label placement is consistent (same side, same offset) across a diagram. | Wong, Labels and callouts 2013 ("the same consistency and alignment … as text"); draw.io blog (consistent label positions); Moody (diagrammatic complexity) | Every crossing is a false intersection the eye must resolve. | **partly** — `arrow-crossing` refuses arrow-through-shape; arrow-arrow crossings and consistent label side are silent (baseline). |

## 6. Data-ink, colour, visual variables

| # | Rule | Source | Rationale | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | Maximise the data-ink ratio: erase non-data ink and redundant data-ink, within reason; every mark must earn its place. | Tufte VDQI ch. 4 (data-ink = "the non-erasable core of a graphic") | Ink that carries no information competes with ink that does. | **in skill** — "containers, sparingly", "titles … need no box", isomorphism test (structure-only must still argue). Not stated as a check: "what could be erased with no loss?" |
| 6.2 | No chartjunk: no decorative texture, moiré hatching, heavy grids, 3-D, or gratuitous colour; grids, if any, are thin and light grey. | Tufte VDQI ch. 5; EI ch. 3 ("noise of 1 + 1 = 3 is proportional to contrast … lighter colors minimise clutter") | Junk adds visual activity that is not information. | **in skill** — hachure = unfinished, roughness 2 banned, grey for scaffolding, three opacity tiers. |
| 6.3 | Smallest effective difference: make every visual distinction as small as it can be and still be clear; loud contrasts are reserved for the point. | Tufte VE ch. 4; EI ch. 5 ("Above all, do no harm"; "colour spots against a light grey or muted field highlight and italicize data"; Imhof: large areas quiet, small areas bright) | Strong differences everywhere leave nothing for emphasis. | **partly** — pale fills with saturated strokes and grey scaffolding follow Imhof; but *every* card takes a role colour by default, so nothing is muted for a bright spot to sit against. |
| 6.4 | Choose the visual variable by what it can encode: **position** and **size** are the only quantitative variables; **value/lightness** and **texture** are ordered but not quantitative (need a legend to read "how much"); **hue**, **shape**, **orientation** are nominal only — hue has no natural order; shape is the one non-selective variable (you cannot scan for "all the hexagons"); size and value are *dissociative* (they dominate and cannot be ignored). | Bertin 1967/1983, Part One (levels of organisation: associative, selective, ordered, quantitative); Roth 2015 (confirms the assignment) | Encoding an ordered quantity in hue, or a category in size, asks the eye to read something it cannot. | **partly** — the skill gets hue right (nominal roles) and uses opacity (a value-like, ordered variable) for the ordered "depth" tiers, which is correct. It never says: do not encode order or amount in hue; do not vary size unless it is a quantity (see 1.4); do not rely on shape for scanning. |
| 6.5 | Use colour to label, measure, imitate reality, or enliven — one of those, knowingly; large background areas muted, small areas saturated; a single hue with lightness steps for an ordered scale, never a rainbow. | Tufte EI ch. 5 (four uses of colour; Imhof's rules; against ROYGBIV maps) | Multi-hue scales create false contours; the eye reads lighter mid-spectrum as "higher". | **in skill** for labelling; **missing** for measuring — a chart panel has no ordered-scale guidance (the type ramp and opacity tiers are the nearest). |
| 6.6 | Show comparisons, causality/mechanism, and multivariate data; integrate words, numbers and images; content counts most. | Tufte BE, Fundamental Principles | The analytical acts a picture exists to enable. | **in skill** — "a diagram argues", before/after, chart with real numbers, "every claim traceable to a line in that code". |
| 6.7 | Graphical integrity: a visual measure is proportional to the number it represents (lie factor ≈ 1); scales are complete; no area/volume for a 1-D quantity. | Tufte VDQI ch. 2 | Distortion misinforms even when the numbers printed are right. | **partly** — "real numbers, with units" and roughness 0 for axes; nothing about proportional bars or unbroken axes. |

## Where the shipped skill contradicts a source

1. **`pass` green vs `fail` red as the essential contrast** — Tufte VDQI ch. 9 names this the unfriendly design; C4 asks for colour-blind and greyscale survival. The palette gate scores luminance only. The two roles need a second channel (border style, glyph, or label) or a hue pair that survives deuteranopia.
2. **Colour roles decodable only by a key that is never drawn** — `palette.md` keeps role names off cards (right, per Tufte) but nothing supplies the key C4 requires. Today a reader cannot learn that purple means "leaves the machine".
3. **`dashed` means two things** — provisional/inferred (patterns.md) and label-leader stroke (authoring.md). Moody's symbol overload; Wong separates leaders by *head*, not by dash.
4. **`null` arrowhead = "relation without direction"** — legitimate for structure, but the skill does not fence it off from relationship lines, where C4/Structurizr forbid it.
5. **Hand-drawn register for finished work** — reads as draft to C4-PlantUML / whiteboard-trained audiences. Keep it, but say the cost and when to drop to `roughness: 0` for an architecture deliverable.
6. **"Consecutive panels take different patterns"** — correct for a teaching band, the inverse of Tufte's small multiples; the skill should carve out the repeated-design case rather than let the rule forbid it.

## Top missing rules, ranked by how often architecture diagrams break them

Ranking uses Brown's workshop counts (title missing on ">90 %" of first drafts; "only around half of the lines are labelled … very common") and the recurrence of each item across the C4 checklist, draw.io, Wong and Tufte.

1. **No diagram title stating type and scope** (2.1) — the most frequent defect Brown reports; the skill has per-frame claims but no diagram-level title convention for sketch or band.
2. **No key/legend for colour, border, head and line-style encodings** (1.1/1.2) — required by C4, and the skill's own unlabelled colour roles make it non-optional.
3. **Unlabelled or generic ("uses") arrows; label not matching direction** (4.2) — half of all lines in Brown's sample; the skill has the mechanism and no requirement.
4. **Mixed abstraction levels in one picture** (3.1) — named by draw.io, C4 and Roos as the classic failure; the skill has "one idea per frame" but no notion of level.
5. **Element size varying without meaning** (1.4/6.4) — text-fitted cards make every layer a false ranking (Bertin: size is ordered and dissociative).
6. **Colour-vision and greyscale failure, red/green as the key contrast** (1.6) — a house-palette defect, not just a silence.
7. **Bidirectional or headless relationship lines** (4.1) — the skill permits `null` heads without saying where they are wrong.
8. **No stated relationship semantics per diagram — dependency vs data flow** (4.3).
9. **Unexplained acronyms** (1.10).
10. **Inconsistent orientation across a set; no "important thing in the middle / entry point" rule for a sketch** (5.2/5.1).
11. **No element type/technology line on architecture elements** (1.9).
12. **No date/source footer** (2.3) — cheap, and the C4 FAQ's staleness point makes it load-bearing for architecture.

Enforceable mechanically today, with what the gate already sees: 2.1 (a title text element exists outside every frame or at the top of a sketch), 1.1 (every `roles` colour present on canvas also appears in a key group, or a `--no-key` opt-out), 4.2 (every `arrow` bound at both ends has a bound label), 4.1 (no arrow with `null` at both ends *bound to two shapes* unless the register declares it), 1.4 (cards sharing a `graph` layer or a `row` differ in size beyond a tolerance → warn), 1.6 (simulate deuteranopia on role pairs in `palette.js` the way the dark theme is already scored). The rest are prose for `patterns.md` / `anti-patterns.md`.
